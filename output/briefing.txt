Melatonin, an endogenous hormone, has been shown to have therapeutic effects on amyloid deposition, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation in AD. However, the mechanisms of its neuroprotective effects in the hippocampus remain largely unknown. This study aimed to investigate the multi-mechanistic neuroprotective effects of melatonin in AD. Eight-month-old female 5xFAD transgenic mice were treated with melatonin (10 mg/kg) for 4 weeks. Temozolomide and bromodeoxyuridine were used to block neurogenesis and to label proliferating neurons, respectively. Our results showed that 5xFAD mice treated with melatonin exhibited improved exploratory behavior, enhanced memory performance, reduced depressive-like symptoms, and decreased levels of stress hormones. At the molecular level, melatonin reduced amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaque accumulation, elevated hippocampal neurogenesis markers (DCX and BrdU), increased Trem2 expression and anti-inflammatory microglial activation, restored serotonergic, dopaminergic, catecholaminergic, and GABAergic neurotransmission, and partially normalized broader hippocampal metabolic pathways. Notably, temozolomide treatment abrogated the beneficial effects of melatonin on memory performance and depressive-like behavior in 5xFAD mice, indicating a neurogenesis-dependent mechanism. Collectively, these findings suggest that melatonin ameliorates behavioral deficits in 5xFAD mice through both neurogenesis-independent (reduced Aβ plaque accumulation, Trem2-microglial activation, neurotransmission restoration, and metabolic remodeling) and hippocampal neurogenesis-dependent mechanisms. Overall, melatonin holds therapeutic potential for attenuating the neuropathology and behavioral deficits in AD.

Neuronal PLA2G2F mediates intercellular signaling to decrease lipid droplet and cholesterol ester load in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived microglia. Our findings implicate PLA2G2F as a compensatory neuroprotective broadcast signal that maintains lipid homeostasis to counteract aging-associated cognitive decline.

While Akkermansia muciniphila (AKK) has shown potential in modulating neuroinflammation, its specific role and underlying mechanisms in alleviating PD-associated non-motor symptoms remain unclear. In this study, we investigated the effects of AKK intervention in 16-month-old female A53T α-synuclein (α-syn) transgenic mice. Behavioral assessments revealed that oral administration of AKK significantly ameliorated depression-like behaviors, evidenced by reduced immobility in the forced swim test and increased sucrose preference, without affecting motor function, spatial memory, or gastrointestinal motility. Mechanistically, the intervention exhibited genotype-specific effects: AKK significantly increased plasma 5-HT levels in C57 mice. This increase was associated with an enrichment of Lactobacillus taiwanensis and metabolic pathways favoring peripheral tryptophan conversion. Conversely, in A53T mice, the treatment specifically elevated hippocampal 5-HT levels without altering plasma concentrations. This central effect correlated with distinct microbial remodeling, characterized by the enrichment of butyrate/propionate-producing Lachnospiraceae and the activation of purine degradation. Collectively, these findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the therapeutic potential of AKK for managing non-motor symptoms in PD. While limited to a female cohort, our results suggest that AKK ameliorates depression-like behaviors in PD through sex-specific remodeling of the gut microbiome and serotonergic signaling.
