Although allo-HSCT-associated gut microbiota dysbiosis has been extensively investigated, longitudinal changes in oral microbiota and their relationship with bloodstream infections (BSIs) remain incompletely understood. We therefore aimed to characterize longitudinal changes in oral and gut microbiota and examine their association with BSIs. METHODS: We prospectively analyzed the oral and gut microbiota of 16 adult allo-HSCT recipients using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Salivary and fecal samples were collected before transplantation, on the day of transplantation, during neutropenia, at engraftment, and after engraftment. RESULTS: Six patients developed 11 BSI episodes, with the first episode in each patient occurring at a median of 8 days after transplantation. Staphylococcus haemolyticus was the most common pathogen (7/11 episodes), followed by Enterococcus faecium (2/11 episodes). The oral microbiota underwent marked longitudinal alterations throughout the transplantation period, accompanied by similar changes in the gut microbiota. Differences in microbiota trajectories were observed between patients with and without BSIs despite comparable cumulative antibiotic exposure. Oral-associated genera, including Streptococcus, Fusobacterium, Veillonella, Actinomyces, and Lautropia, were detected in fecal samples, suggesting possible oral-to-gut translocation. CONCLUSIONS: The oral and gut microbiota underwent substantial longitudinal alterations during allo-HSCT. Differences in microbiota dynamics between patients with and without BSIs were not explained solely by antibiotic exposure. These exploratory findings provide insights into the timing of oral and gut microbiota alterations during allo-HSCT and highlight potential relationships between oral and gut microbial changes and BSIs.

Validating the nonbioenergetic mitochondrial stressors as independent pathogenic factors is challenging because most mouse models of mitochondrial stress are confounded by bioenergetic defects. Here, we show that expression of Ant1P.A114P,A123D, previously shown to cause mitochondrial protein import clogging in cultured human cells, does not affect the overall bioenergetic capacity, yet it induces proteostatic stress responses in the cytosol that suggests accumulation of unimported precursor proteins. These data suggest that Ant1P.A114P,A123D likely induces mitochondrial protein import stress, and contributes to neural damage through cytosolic proteostatic stress and altered aggregation dynamics of neuropathogenic proteins independent of bioenergetics.
