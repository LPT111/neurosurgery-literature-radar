Retrospective studies in patients with nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer have reported a significant reduction in Alzheimer's disease incidence (12 to 78%) among Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) recipients versus controls. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we evaluated BCG in the PS19 mouse model of tauopathy. We found that BCG administration reduced hippocampal phospho-tau and microgliosis while preserving neuronal markers. In vivo volumetric T2-magnetic resonance Imaging (MRI) demonstrated attenuation of brain atrophy accompanied by increased glutamate-weighted chemical exchange saturation transfer-MRI signals. Functionally, BCG-treated mice showed improved performance in the novel object recognition test, as well as improved body-weight maintenance and survival. Transcriptomic profiling of the hippocampus revealed near complete normalization of the PS19 disease-associated gene expression signature toward that of healthy controls. Flow cytometric profiling of brain myeloid populations demonstrated a reduction in activated resident microglia, but total microglia cells remain elevated. Moreover, an increase of trained immunity marker CD80 on the recruited peripheral myeloid cells ensues following BCG treatment. Consistent with this shift in myeloid state, primary brain myeloid cells from BCG-treated mice also exhibited enhanced phagocytosis of fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled tau fibrils and increased lactate production. Together, these findings indicate that BCG induces systemic and central nervous system myeloid cell reprogramming that limits neuroinflammation, enhances tau clearance, and rescues cognitive and neurodegenerative phenotypes in a tauopathy model. BCG is a safe, readily available therapy that merits consideration as a preventive agent against dementia.

Brain inflammation is a key feature of frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Positron emission tomography (PET) of the 18 kDa translocator protein (TSPO) is widely used as an in vivo proxy for neuroinflammation, but whether the elevated signal reflects microglial, astrocytic or vascular pathology remains controversial, and this has not previously been resolved in FTD using matched ante mortem imaging and post-mortem tissue. Total tissue TSPO-area fraction was likewise increased in FTD and tracked regional PET binding in two independently processed slide sets. The elevated TSPO-PET signal in FTD, therefore, likely reflects an increased burden of CD68+ phagocytic microglia rather than TSPO expression in other cell types, supporting TSPO-PET as a microglial-burden biomarker across both FTLD-tau and FTLD-TDP.

Chemotherapy-induced cognitive impairment (CICI) affects many cancer survivors, who often report cognitive difficulties during and/or after treatment that reduce quality of life. While the exact mechanisms that underlie CICI are unclear, neuroinflammation is widely proposed to contribute. One candidate mediator is the nucleotide-binding domain leucine-rich repeat and pyrin domain containing receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, which drives microglial inflammation and is implicated in Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease. This study therefore investigated whether NLRP3 inflammasome components are altered in a rat model of CICI. Female Sprague Dawley rats were randomly allocated to receive methotrexate (MTX), 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) or saline (n = 18/treatment) and were euthanised at 96-hours, 31-days or 93-days post-treatment (n = 6/treatment/timepoint). This cohort was previously shown to develop cognitive deficits. In the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, NLRP3 and pro-caspase-1 were measured by western blot, and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) by ELISA. The repeated failures of anti-amyloid therapies in Alzheimer's disease (AD) have exposed critical limitations of the linear amyloid cascade hypothesis and underscored the need for a broader etiological framework. The objective of this review is to synthesise mechanistic evidence for an exposome‑based model of sporadic AD that integrates metabolic, inflammatory, and sleep/circadian contributions to brain aging. Epidemiological evidence indicates that up to 40% of AD risk is attributable to modifiable environmental and lifestyle factors. These factors, collectively termed the aging exposome, represent the cumulative measure of external exposures and the body's biological responses across the lifespan. The central hypothesis is that sporadic AD emerges from the synergistic convergence of three interconnected exposome domains, metabolic dyshomeostasis, chronic neuroinflammation, and sleep/circadian disruption, which collectively lower the threshold for clinical dementia through shared molecular hubs like NF‑κB, mTOR, and ER stress.
